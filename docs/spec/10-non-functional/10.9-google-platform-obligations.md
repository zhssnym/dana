### 10.9 Google platform obligations

Dana signs people in with Google and nothing else (10.1, 11.6), so Google's developer
terms bind the product. This section records what they require, what Dana already does,
and the one thing they require that Dana does not do. **Checked against the primary
documents on 2026-09-03** (issue #25); each document's own "last modified" date is given,
because that, not the date of this check, is what tells a later reader whether the ground
moved.

Three documents govern, and only three:

- the [Google API Services User Data
  Policy](https://developers.google.com/terms/api-services-user-data-policy), last updated
  15 February 2024, which covers Google Sign-In by name;
- the [Google APIs Terms of Service](https://developers.google.com/terms/), last modified
  9 November 2021;
- the OAuth app verification requirements, in the [OAuth App Verification Help
  Center](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/13463073) and its [verification
  requirements](https://support.google.com/cloud/answer/13464321) page.

**Deletion is not one of the obligations, and the belief that it was is now retired.** 8.10
used to end "Google requires apps offering account creation to offer deletion; verify the
current policy text before launch", the check was never done at launch on 2026-08-16, and
the sentence turns out to name the wrong rulebook. The User Data Policy states no
account-deletion requirement and no data-export requirement of any kind; nor do the
verification requirements. The rule it half-remembered is **Google Play's**: the Play
Console's [account deletion
requirements](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13327111)
oblige an app that offers in-app account creation to offer both an in-app deletion path and
a **web link** at which deletion can be requested without reinstalling. That policy binds
apps distributed on Google Play. Dana is a PWA on no store, so it does not apply, and Dana
implements deletion anyway (8.10, behind 9.7's typed confirmation, described on the Privacy
page). **If Dana is ever wrapped and listed on Play or the App Store, this paragraph stops
being true and the web deletion link becomes work**, which is why it is written down rather
than left as an absence.

**What the User Data Policy does require, all of it met.** A published privacy policy that
fully documents how the application accesses, uses, stores and shares Google user data,
with its URL listed in the OAuth client configuration; only the minimum scopes the product
needs; no deceptive or undisclosed use; and user data secure in transit and at rest. Dana's
Privacy page names the three Google-derived fields, what they are used for, and who they
are shown to (9.2 and its copy), and 10.1 fixes the scopes at `openid email profile`.

**Brand verification, and the two facts this repository cannot check.** The verification
requirements name four clauses that bind an app accessing Google APIs: a homepage on a
domain the developer has verified, describing the product rather than being only a login
page; a privacy policy hosted on that same domain, linked from the homepage and from the
OAuth consent screen and **identical in both places**; verified ownership of every
authorized domain, through Search Console; and Google's sign-in branding on the button
that starts the flow. Dana meets the first two by construction, one origin serving `/`,
`/privacy` and `/terms` (9.2, 11.1, AC-240). The other two are facts about the Cloud project
rather than about this repository, and **must be confirmed by whoever administers it**: that
the authorized domain is verified, and that the consent screen's privacy URL is the same URL
the landing footer links.

**Scope class decides everything else, and Dana is in the mildest class.** `openid`, `email`
and `profile` are non-sensitive, so the Limited Use restrictions, the annual security
assessment and the annual re-verification that the User Data Policy attaches to Sensitive
and Restricted scopes do not reach Dana. Submitting for verification is correspondingly not
mandatory — with two consequences that bear on opening Dana up. An unverified app cannot
show its name and logo on the consent screen, which needs the lighter "brand verification"
submission. And Google's own text puts the ceiling for an unverified app at **100 users**,
"such apps will need to complete a verification, if they want to grow their user base beyond
100". **Whether that ceiling applies to a non-sensitive-scope app already published in
production is ambiguous in Google's wording**, which states the 100-user cap plainly for
apps in testing or staging publishing status and states it for "personal use" apps without
tying it to a scope class. The safe reading is that verification is the gate on growth past
100 accounts, and the cheap move is to submit for brand verification before Dana is opened
up rather than to discover the cap by hitting it. Requesting any scope beyond the three
above changes this paragraph entirely and is a decision, not an implementation detail.

**One obligation Dana does not meet, and it is in the APIs Terms of Service rather than in
either policy.** Section 3(d), "Data Portability": "For as long as you use or store any user
data that you obtained through the APIs, you agree to enable your users to export their
equivalent data to other services or applications of their choice in a way that's
substantially as fast and easy as exporting such data from Google products and services,
subject to applicable laws". Dana obtains a name, an email address and a picture through
Google Sign-In and stores them (4.1), and offers no export surface at all: no endpoint in
section 7, no control in section 9. **"Equivalent data" is genuinely ambiguous.** The narrow
reading is the Google-derived profile fields alone, which a person can already read on their
own Settings page; the wide reading is everything Dana holds about that person, tasks
included. **The safe reading is the wide one**, because the clause measures Dana against
how easily Google itself hands the same data over, and Google's own answer is Takeout: a
file, not a screen. That is the reading to build to when it is built, and it is not built
here. An export surface has a shape: a format, an authorization gate, a rate limit, a size
bound. Inventing one inside a compliance note would be the wrong way to acquire it. **This
paragraph exists so that the absence reads as a known gap with an owner rather than as an
oversight; the work itself belongs to an issue.**
