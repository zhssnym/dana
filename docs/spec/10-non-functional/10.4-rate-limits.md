### 10.4 Rate limits

Per user: invites 5 per minute and 20 per day; invite resend 1 per invite per 24 hours;
search 30 requests per minute; general mutations 120 per minute; data export 5 per hour;
all others unlimited in
v1. Responses use 429 plus Retry-After. Enforcement is per deployment instance in v1
(single-instance hosting per section 11); revisit if horizontally scaled.

**The data export row was added 2026-09-04 with 7.22 (issue #77), and it is the first
limit in this section on a read.** Every other GET in the API answers one page of one
surface; that one walks nine tables for one person and builds the whole document in
memory before a byte goes out, so the ground for the limit is cost rather than abuse.
Five per hour is far above what the act is for — a person exports when they are leaving,
or once out of curiosity — and far below what it would take to make the endpoint a way of
loading the process. It is refused exactly as the sentence above says every limit is
refused: 429 with Retry-After and the 7.0 envelope.

Added 2026-09-04 (issue #99): **CSP violation reports, 30 per minute, keyed on the
caller's address and not on a user.** It is the one row here that cannot be per user,
because the caller is a browser posting a report of its own accord (10.8) and has no
session to key on. Thirty is chosen from both ends: a real breakage on a cold load
produces one report per refused resource and the shell loads a handful, so thirty leaves
a wide margin above the case the endpoint exists to catch; and thirty bounds what a
stranger can spend of the free-tier Sentry quota 11.12 sizes for ordinary error volume,
which is the case it must not become. Nothing in this row should be read as identifying a
visitor.

**The two sentences that stood here about `trust proxy` are corrected by issue #153 later
the same day, and the correction is recorded rather than swept, because what they said was
true of this deployment for as long as it has had a per-address limit.** They said that
this process does not set Express's `trust proxy`, so behind the edge of 11.11 the address
it reads is the edge's and the bucket is per deployment rather than per visitor; that this
was the conservative direction; and, plainly, that thirty reports a minute from anywhere
would then hold the endpoint at 429 for everyone. All of that was accurate, and the last
of it is why #153 was raised rather than deferred: "conservative" is not the word for a
limit a stranger can use to silence every visitor's browser at once. They also said that
changing `trust proxy` would move every limiter above and was not a change to make inside
that row, which is exactly what happened — it was made from outside it.

**So, as of 2026-09-04 and issue #153: every limit in this section is per client, and
behind the edge that means per client and not per deployment.** `createApp` sets `trust
proxy` to `1`, exactly one hop, so the address a limiter keys on is the one Render's edge
appended to `X-Forwarded-For` — whoever opened the connection to it — rather than the edge
itself. Two things this assumes of the hosting, both of them 11.11's to keep true. There
is **exactly one** proxy in front of this process, Render's edge, which terminates TLS and
opens its own socket, and it appends the caller to that header. **That second half is
near-certain rather than documented, and 11.11 records what was checked and what follows if
it is wrong. What follows is not neutral.** If the edge appends nothing, then nothing
overwrites a header a caller writes for themselves, and these limits go from one over-strict
shared bucket to **no bucket at all**, a stranger rotating the value meeting no limit. So the
production check 11.11 names — read `req.ip` once against the deployed service — is what
settles this row, and until it is run "per client" is a strong inference rather than a
measurement. And nothing else may be put in front of it without moving the
number, because the count is a claim about the deployment: a CDN ahead of Render makes it
`2`. **The number is never
`true`**, which would trust the whole header, and the whole header is written by the
client — a caller could then present a fresh address per request and meet no limit at all.
AC-410 measures all of it against the running application: one forwarded address, two, and
none, and two clients not sharing a bucket.

The per-user limits above are unaffected either way, keying on the session rather than on
an address; the CSP row is the only one keyed on an address today, and the rest of this
section's numbers therefore mean what they always said. What has changed is what a
*future* per-address limit means, and it now means what its row will say.
